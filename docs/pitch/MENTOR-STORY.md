# The mentor story

**Five minutes. One family. No screens, no steps, no app.**

This is for the mentor round, where nobody sees the product. The job is for them
to walk away understanding what we built, why it exists, and why it is hard —
from a story alone.

Read it out loud three times before you present. Not to memorise it. To find the
places where you stop sounding like yourself.

---

## Before you start

**Two rules for the whole five minutes.**

The first: **say nothing you cannot defend.** If a mentor asks "how do you know?"
about any sentence here, you have an answer, and it is written in the margins of
this document. That is the whole reason to prepare.

The second: **talk about one family the entire time.** The temptation, as the
clock runs, is to widen out — the market, the opportunity, the crore of people.
Don't. One family, named, with ages. A mentor who remembers Prabha will remember
Parcha. A mentor who hears about four hundred million outpatient visits will
remember nothing.

**Words to keep out of your mouth.** Platform. Solution. Ecosystem. Seamless.
Leverage. Empower. Journey. Pipeline. Robust. End-to-end. Holistic.
AI-powered. Game-changer. Revolutionise. Disrupt. Any sentence that starts with
"So basically". Any sentence with the word "basically" in it at all.

If you catch yourself about to say "our solution", say "it" or "Parcha" or "the
app". If you catch yourself about to say "the patient", say "Prabha".

---

## The script

> Timings are for a 150-word-a-minute delivery, which is slower than you think
> you speak. The whole thing is about 720 words. Practise with a timer; if you
> finish in four minutes you are rushing, and rushing is what loses this round.

### 0:00 — Prabha

*(Start here. No title slide, no team introduction, no "we are team Afterburners".
If they want to know who you are they will ask. Start with her.)*

Prabha Devi is seventy-one. She lives in Rohini, in north Delhi, with her son
Prashant and his wife Priya. One phone in the house. Her grandson set it up.

In October she had a fever that would not go. They took her to the hospital on a
Tuesday. They left the house at seven in the morning. She saw the doctor at
about half past ten. He was with her for four minutes, maybe five. He was good.
He examined her properly, he asked the right questions, and he wrote her a
prescription.

Four medicines. And at the bottom, in his handwriting, perfectly clear:
**1-0-1 × 5 days. HS. SOS.**

*(Pause here. Let it sit. Then, quieter:)*

By Thursday she was taking two of the four. By Sunday, one. Not because she is
careless. Because nobody in that house could tell her what **HS** meant.

### 0:50 — Why that happens

Here is the part that surprised us, and it changed what we built.

We assumed the problem was doctors' handwriting. Everyone assumes that. It is
the joke everybody makes.

It is not true. There is a study from a government hospital in Kolkata, three
hundred and eighty patients coming out of the outpatient department, published
last year. Ninety-six per cent of those prescriptions were perfectly legible.
Eighty-six per cent were complete by the national guidelines. The doctors had
done their job.

And thirty-seven per cent of those patients did not understand what they had
been given. On *frequency* — how many times a day — only four in ten got it
right. Women did worse than men. Over-fifties did worse than under-fifties. The
poorer you were, the worse you did.

So the handwriting is fine. The prescription is correct. And the care still
fails, about ten feet outside the consulting room door.

*(Beat.)*

And when the researchers asked those patients what would actually help, they
asked for two things. Write it in our language. And show us pictures.

That is not our idea. That is what the patients asked for. We just went and
built it.

### 1:50 — The other half

There is a second thing wrong with Prabha's Tuesday, and it has nothing to do
with medicine.

She waited three and a half hours for four minutes. The government's own
auditors looked at outpatient departments in Jammu and Kashmir and found most
consultations were five minutes or less, and most patients waited more than
thirty. In one hospital study the average wait was a hundred and ten minutes for
a five-minute consultation.

Nobody was hiding anything from her. There was simply no way for her to know,
from her house, that she should leave at nine instead of seven.

*(Then turn it around — this is the turn that makes the clinic side land:)*

And now stand where the doctor is standing. Sixty patients before lunch. Two or
three minutes each. He has been writing prescriptions by hand for twenty-two
years. His hand is faster than his typing will ever be.

So every piece of software anyone has ever sold him makes his day *worse*. He
tries it for a week. Then he goes back to the pad, and a boy at the front desk
types it all in again in the evening, from the carbon copy, so the records
exist.

That is the real reason clinics don't go digital. Not cost. Not internet. The
software costs the doctor time he does not have.

### 3:00 — What we made

So Parcha has to do two things at once, and they pull in opposite directions.

**For the doctor, it has to be faster than his pen.** He holds down the space
bar and talks the way he already talks — half English, half Hindi, "paracetamol
650 do baar, paanch din, khaane ke baad". It lands in the right boxes. He reads
it, he fixes what is wrong, he signs. He is not typing. He is not learning
anything. On a bad line, with the internet down, it still works, because the
listening happens on the computer in front of him and nothing is waiting on a
network.

**And for Prabha, it has to arrive in a form she can actually use.** So when
Prashant gets handed the slip, there is a square code printed on it. He points the
phone at it, and her medicines appear on the screen — in Hindi, as pictures. A
sun for the morning, a moon for the night, the actual number of tablets drawn
out. No "1-0-1". No "HS". It reads itself out loud if she taps it. And at eight
in the evening her phone rings, and a voice in Hindi says which tablet to take
and asks her to press one when she has taken it.

The same phone holds her son's medicines and her daughter-in-law's, kept apart,
each in whichever language that person reads. Prabha's screen is one big
instruction at a time. Her grandson's is a normal app. Same phone.

*(If you have a few seconds spare, this is the one extra sentence worth adding:)*

And her son can take that slip to any chemist in the market, exactly as he
always has, because it is still a piece of paper.

### 4:15 — The one decision we would defend

*(This is the part that separates you from the other teams. Do not cut it.)*

We made one choice that sounds backwards, and it is the one we are proudest of.

Her prescription does not travel over the internet. It travels on the paper.

The whole thing — every medicine, the dose, the timing, the doctor's
registration number, the hospital — is squeezed into that printed square and
signed by the hospital when the doctor signs. Her phone opens it and checks the
signature by itself, with the data turned off, in a village with one bar.

Which means there is no server in the middle to be down, no link to expire, and
nothing about Prabha's fever sitting on anybody's cloud waiting to be fetched.
The paper she has held in her hand for fifty years is the thing carrying the
data.

That felt like the honest answer for the country we are building this in.

### 4:45 — Where we actually are

I want to be straight with you about this, because you will ask.

It is built and it runs. Four apps you can open right now — the clinic, the
family's phone, an Android build, a Windows program for a front desk. A hundred
and fifty-seven tests pass. The whole visit works with the internet unplugged.

And: nobody has used it yet. No real patient, no real clinic. The reminder calls
work against a test phone line, not a live one. We have not connected to the
government's health-ID system, though the records are already shaped for it.

What we have is a thing that works and has never met a real Tuesday. What we are
asking for is help getting it in front of one doctor and one family, so we can
find out which half of this we got wrong.

*(Stop. Don't add anything. Let them ask.)*

## The questions they will ask, and what to say

**"How is this different from Practo?"**

Practo sells the doctor a better desk. Appointments, records, billing,
prescriptions — and it is good at that. But when the visit is over, Practo's
job is finished. The patient gets a text message with a link.

Our whole argument is that the visit being over is where the problem starts.
Parcha is the only one of these that treats the grandmother as the user. Her
language, her picture of a tablet, her phone call at eight o'clock. Nobody is
building for her because she does not pay.

Then stop. Don't list features. The grandmother line is the answer.

**"Isn't this the Ayushman card?"** *(This is the question we got wrong last time.
Say this almost word for word.)*

No — and they are two different cards, which trips a lot of people up.

The **Ayushman card** is insurance. Five lakh rupees a year of free hospital
treatment for families who qualify. It pays the bill. It has nothing to say
about medicines or instructions.

The **ABHA number** is the other one, and that is the one worth comparing us to.
It is a health ID — ninety-four crore of them exist, over a hundred crore
records attached. It is how one hospital finds the records another hospital made.

And it is good, and we want to plug into it. But notice what it is *for*. It
moves records between institutions. There is nothing in it that puts a picture
of a white tablet in front of a seventy-one-year-old woman in Hindi and reads it
out to her. A hundred crore records linked and Prabha still didn't know what HS
meant.

ABHA is the railway. We are trying to be something worth putting on it.

**"Why not WhatsApp? Everyone has WhatsApp."**

Because the moment her prescription goes through a chat app, it is on somebody
else's servers, and it needs a network to arrive. The code on the paper needs
neither. And a photo in a WhatsApp forward does not ring her phone at eight and
wait for her to press one.

**"What is the AI actually doing?"**

Listening, and nothing else — on the computer in the room, not in a data centre.
Where the medicines go on the page is decided by rules we wrote and can read out
to you line by line, because a prescription is not the place for a program that
is right most of the time. And the thing that checks for allergies and clashes
has no AI in it at all, on purpose. If it flags something, it can tell you
exactly which record it read.

We were strict about this. The doctor's own words, and plain rules. Nothing that
invents a medicine.

**"Who pays?"**

The clinic, monthly. Not the family — if Prabha had to pay, this doesn't work.
Honest answer on the number: we have a price on a slide and no evidence behind
it. That is one of the things we want to find out from a pilot.

## Delivery notes

- **Do not bring the laptop out.** If you open a screen in this round you will
  start demonstrating, and demonstrating is the other round. Hands empty.
- **Slow down at 0:00 and 0:50.** Those two paragraphs do all the work. Everything
  after them is detail.
- **The pause after "HS" is the most important second in the pitch.** Count two.
  It is uncomfortable. Do it anyway.
- **Say the numbers like a person.** "Thirty-seven per cent", not "36.8%". "About
  three and a half hours", not "210 minutes". You only need exact figures if
  challenged.
- **If you are running long, cut the 1:50 section** (the wait, the doctor's
  twenty-two years) down to two sentences. Never cut 0:00, 0:50 or 4:15.
- **If they interrupt, stop and answer.** A mentor interrupting is a mentor
  interested. Do not fight to get back to the script; the script is not the
  point.
- **End on the ask, not on a feature.** The last thing out of your mouth should
  be that you want this in front of one real doctor and one real family.
